Polymarket Steady Climbers

I scanned 149,451 Polymarket wallets for P&L charts like yours: a steady climb, no big drawdowns. 543 qualified. This page shows what those traders actually do, which "copy them" ideas fail when tested on the whole market, and the one boring rule set that held up in both halves of the year.

The strategy

Deadline Fade: bet that the deadline passes quietly

Most "Will X happen by <date>?" markets end with nothing happening. When the quiet side trades at 95–98¢ with one to four weeks left, it has paid slightly more than its price implies, in both halves of the last year. Each trade earns a little; spread across many small positions, it builds the kind of curve you showed.

    Expect modest returns, not a fortune. The backtest made about 9% a year with limit orders (about 6% paying the spread and fees), with a worst drawdown near 2%. One loss wipes out about 27 wins, so position size is the whole game.

    Study

    What the 543 actually do

    I pulled 956,239 of their positions plus their last 3,000 trades each, tagged every market, and sorted each trader into one style. The largest group is the boring one: buying outcomes priced around 90–99¢ shortly before they resolve. Market makers are second and earn the most in total.

    Test

    A steady chart does predict more profit

    I picked steady climbers using only the first half of the year (Nov 2025 – May 2026), then looked at the second half. The pick still beats equally profitable but bumpy traders, so these curves reflect a real edge rather than luck. Profits do shrink: the median trader kept about a third of the earlier pace.

    Test

    Three shortcuts that lose money

    Before building a strategy, I tested the obvious ways to copy these traders on every market that resolved in the last year, using realistic fills (half a tick + 0.2¢ slippage) and the fee schedule that starts on 20 November.

    1 · Buying favourites at 90–99¢

    132,586 markets, first time the favourite enters each price band within 3 days of its end date. Favourites win less often than their price implies.

    2 · Copying the steady wallets

    Wallets chosen as steady on the first half only; their second-half positions copied at the price one hour after they bought.

    3 · Stop-losses

    Selling when the favourite falls below a level made every version of the strategy worse. Scares usually pass, and a stop sells at the worst price.

    So how do the bond buyers win? Two ways a price chart can't show. About a third of their money goes in after the event's scheduled end, at ~99.5¢, once they have checked the official result (99–100% won). The biggest slice is "nothing ever happens" bets placed weeks before a deadline, such as "Trump out by June 30?" No at 96¢. That second slice is the part that can be turned into rules, and that is the strategy below.

      Fees change on 20 November
